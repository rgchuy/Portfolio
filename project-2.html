Queue the music, download once

How a download list helped new users of a free music app build their offline library in a single short Wi-Fi visit, making it easier to keep using the app.

Long story short

TREBEL is a free, legal music app that doesn’t stream: songs are downloaded and kept on the phone. Brands pay through ads shown only while songs download.

The company struggled to retain new users and had no clear picture of their context or why they left the app, so research was needed to understand them.

Data was used to recruit the right participants for interviews and usability tests, giving a clear view of their lifestyle, constraints and experience with the app.

Based on the findings, the download experience was redesigned while preserving the core business needs, adding a new Download Queue that increased new-user retention by 30%.

The download climb

A hard climb

Downloading songs one by one, with an ad before each, felt like climbing a mountain. Discouraged by the effort it required, many new users gave up halfway. Those who made it through went on to stay and enjoy the app.

The blind spot

“Downloading songs isn’t is a retention problem.”

With teams spread across five countries, the download step was judged from the USA team’s point of view, used to cheap and reliable mobile data. It had not been checked against how people in Mexico actually get online.

The assumption USA team view

Data plans are cheap and always on. Downloading 10 songs is a small step before the good part.

vs

The reality Mexico

One of the most expensive mobile-service markets. Every minute online counts. Many users rely on prepaid plans, where each small top-up buys only a few GB of data.

Learnings

Context beats assumptions

A download step that seemed minor from the USA team’s point of view became a barrier in Mexico, where prepaid data is costly and free Wi-Fi visits are short. The context of users in each location has to be reviewed every time.

Respect the user’s time

Speed wasn’t a nice-to-have. For these users, time online was the scarcest thing they had.
